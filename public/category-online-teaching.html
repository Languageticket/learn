Online Teaching

Using AI in online English learning without outsourcing the learning

AI can generate prompts and feedback-like suggestions. It cannot decide what a learner understands, values or needs next.

Published September 4, 2026 · 8 min read
Online Teaching

Generative AI can produce example dialogues, suggest alternative wording and help teachers create practice materials. Those uses are not the same as learning. A learner still needs to notice language, make choices and practise expressing a meaning.

Keep the learner’s thinking visible

A productive workflow starts with the learner’s own attempt. An AI tool can then offer alternatives or questions, but the learner should compare them, explain a choice and produce a revised version. This keeps the work anchored in language use rather than answer collection.

Important note

UNESCO’s guidance calls for human-centred, age-appropriate approaches to generative AI in education and highlights privacy and ethical validation. Treat AI output as material to evaluate, not an authority to obey.

Do not use AI to erase assessment evidence

If the goal is to understand what a learner can do independently, they need an opportunity to perform without external generation. AI-supported practice can sit beside, rather than replace, human observation and authentic performance.

A useful teacher prompt

Ask an AI system to create three contrasting role-play situations at a chosen CEFR level, then review every instruction, language model and cultural assumption before use. The teacher remains responsible for accuracy, appropriateness and learning value.

Designing online speaking practice that learners actually want to repeat

The strongest online speaking tasks give learners a reason to listen, decide and respond.

Published September 4, 2026 · 7 min read
Online Teaching

Online speaking practice does not need to imitate a physical classroom. It needs a clear social purpose, a manageable turn structure and an outcome worth reporting back.

Give conversation an information gap

When each learner holds a different piece of useful information, listening becomes necessary. Use brief, visible prompts and leave enough silence for planning.

Practical application

Ask pairs to choose the best option for a client with three constraints. Each learner receives different information. Their final recommendation becomes the shared outcome.

Online Teaching

A learner’s system for self-regulated online English study

Online study asks learners to make more decisions about time, attention and help. A simple system can make those decisions easier.

Published September 2, 2026 · 8 min read
Online Teaching

Independent online study can look flexible from the outside. In practice, it asks learners to decide when to start, what deserves attention, how long to persist and when to ask for help. Those are learnable behaviours, not fixed personality traits.

Use a short plan–monitor–adjust cycle

  • Plan: choose one concrete outcome for this session.
  • Monitor: pause after a task and ask what you can now do without looking.
  • Adjust: choose a small next step based on the answer.

For English, an outcome might be “leave a 45-second voice note describing yesterday” rather than “study past simple”. The first gives you a visible result and makes feedback easier to use.

Evidence

A systematic review of online higher education studies found positive associations between academic outcomes and strategies including time management, metacognition, effort regulation and critical thinking. The evidence is correlational and comes from a particular population, so it is better read as useful direction than a guarantee.

Make help-seeking specific

“I do not understand grammar” is hard to answer. Save one sentence, say what you intended to mean, and explain what you already tried. This makes a teacher’s response more useful and helps you notice the difference the next time.

How to use educational videos without turning learners into spectators

A good video can model language and focus attention. Learning still needs a task before, during or after viewing.

Published September 2, 2026 · 7 min read
Online Teaching

Video can bring gesture, facial expression, sound and context into an online English course. Its weakness is equally clear: it can invite passive watching. The instructional question is not “Should we use video?” but “What will learners do with it?”

Use a viewing purpose

Before playing a clip, ask learners to listen for a decision, collect language for softening a request, or compare two speakers’ responses. Afterwards, make them use that observation in a short spoken or written task.

Evidence

Guo, Kim and Rubin analysed 6.9 million video-watching sessions in four MOOCs and reported stronger engagement with shorter videos. This is engagement evidence from a particular MOOC context, not a universal rule for every language video or learner.

Segment for the language task

Pause at a communicative point rather than every few seconds. A useful sequence might be: predict the response, watch one exchange, notice two phrases, then role-play the situation with a changed detail.

Common misconception

A polished video is not automatically a better lesson. Clarity, relevance and the task around the video matter more than production value alone.

Online Teaching

Online assessment for language learning: collect evidence, not just clicks

A completed quiz can show something. It rarely shows everything a learner can do with English.

Published September 2, 2026 · 9 min read
Online Teaching

Online assessment is often reduced to a score because scores are easy to collect. For language learning, a stronger question is: what evidence would persuade us that a learner can understand, interact or produce language in a stated context?

Match the evidence to the outcome

If the outcome is to clarify a problem in a customer call, use a short role-play recording or a live exchange—not only a multiple-choice grammar exercise. Discrete-item practice still has a place, especially for focused diagnosis, but it should not stand in for communicative performance.

If you need evidence of…Consider…
Listening for key detailA short clip with an explanation or response
Spoken interactionA paired task with a simple rubric
Writing for a purposeA draft, feedback and revised version
Language formA focused low-stakes quiz plus use in context

Design for transparency and revision

Tell learners what is being assessed, show an example where appropriate, and make room for feedback. A one-shot high-stakes task can be necessary in some systems, but it is not the only way to see progress.

Teaching implication

Keep an assessment record that includes performance samples, not only scores. A sequence of short recordings can make progress audible to both learner and teacher.

Online Teaching

Make online English learning more accessible before a learner has to ask

Accessibility is not an add-on to online teaching. It is part of whether learners can take part in the task at all.

Published September 2, 2026 · 7 min read
Online Teaching

A learner can be present in an online lesson and still be unable to participate fully. Audio quality, bandwidth, screen size, captioning, task timing, unfamiliar tools and assumptions about home space all shape access.

Offer more than one sensible route through the task

For a listening task, provide a transcript after the first attempt when this supports the learning aim. For a speaking task, allow planning notes and a second recording where the aim is improvement rather than speed. For live work, write key instructions in the chat as well as saying them aloud.

Keep the language load intentional

Clear instructions are not “simplifying” a challenging task. They protect the challenge that actually matters. Separate the technical steps from the language problem learners need to solve.

Practical application

Before each new tool, ask: does it add a learning opportunity that our existing tools cannot? If not, remove the setup burden.

Inclusion is also social

Make turn-taking visible, give learners time to prepare and avoid treating camera use as the only signal of engagement. A respectful learning community lets learners contribute in ways that fit the task and their circumstances.

Online Teaching

How to design an online English course around learning, not content coverage

A course becomes teachable when its lessons, practice and evidence of progress point in the same direction.

Published August 31, 2026 · 9 min read
Online Teaching

A list of grammar points is not yet a course design. Learners need to know what they are working towards and to encounter enough guided practice to reach it. In an online setting, this alignment also prevents a common problem: a crowded platform with no clear route through it.

Work backwards from a communicative outcome

Write an outcome that describes a performance in context. “Use past simple” is content; “tell a colleague about a completed project and answer one follow-up question” is a performance. The latter makes choices about language, task design and assessment easier.

Teaching example

For a B1 unit on arranging plans, the final task could be a short voice-note exchange that proposes, negotiates and confirms a time. Earlier activities then prepare the phrases and listening moves the task requires.

Build a repeatable lesson arc

  • Orient: make the purpose and success criteria visible.
  • Notice: use a short text, recording or model to focus attention.
  • Try: give supported practice with a limited demand.
  • Use: ask learners to communicate for a reason.
  • Reflect: use feedback and a second attempt.
Important note

The 2010 evidence review of online learning is often quoted as proof that online is “better”. Its authors caution that blended interventions frequently differed from comparison conditions in more than one way. Design quality matters.

Online Teaching

What live online English lessons are for—and what they are not

Use synchronous time for interaction, coaching and shared thinking; move work that benefits from pause and replay elsewhere.

Published August 31, 2026 · 8 min read
Online Teaching

A live online lesson is costly in attention: everyone must arrive at the same time, manage audio and video, and make quick decisions in front of others. It should therefore do work that benefits from being together.

Prioritise interaction that changes the response

Good live tasks create a reason to listen. A pair task can require learners to compare different information, choose a solution, prepare a recommendation or respond to what a partner has actually said. Asking every learner the same display question is less likely to create that need.

Teaching implication

Give breakout tasks a visible outcome, a time limit and a simple way back into the whole group: one decision, one example, or one question the pair could not resolve.

Make teacher presence visible

Teaching presence is more than being on camera. It includes explaining the purpose, sequencing activity, monitoring understanding and helping learners make use of feedback. A meta-analysis of Community of Inquiry studies found a moderate positive association between teaching presence and actual learning, but it does not establish a single best live-lesson formula.

What to move out of the call

  • First exposure to a replayable explanation.
  • Long individual reading or drafting.
  • Private reflection after feedback.
  • Preparation learners can complete at their own pace.

Online Teaching

Asynchronous discussion is not a quieter version of class

When learners have time to plan, draft and respond, discussion can support different kinds of language work.

Published August 31, 2026 · 7 min read
Online Teaching

Asynchronous discussion gives learners something a live conversation cannot always offer: time. That time can support planning, dictionary use, rereading and a more considered response. It does not, however, make a prompt meaningful by itself.

Design for an audience and a decision

“Write your opinion” often produces parallel posts. Instead, give learners a shared problem and ask them to build on, challenge or rank one another’s ideas. The response should change what happens next.

Teaching example

Give small groups three options for improving a local service. Each learner posts one recommendation with a reason, then replies to two classmates by asking for evidence or proposing a compromise. The group publishes one final choice.

Move beyond exploration

A review of cognitive presence research describes a recurring pattern in online discussion: exploration is common, while integration and resolution are harder to reach. Teachers can help by asking learners to connect two contributions, justify a decision, or apply an idea to a new case.

Common misconception

A high post count is not reliable evidence of good discussion. Look for whether learners are making and revising meaning.

Online Teaching

Feedback in online language learning: design the next attempt

The most useful feedback is connected to a task learners can still revisit, repair or extend.

Published August 31, 2026 · 8 min read
Online Teaching

Online tools make it easy to leave comments, record audio notes or correct text. They do not guarantee that a learner will understand the point, know what to do next or have an opportunity to use it. Feedback design starts with that next move.

Choose one focus that fits the task

A short speaking recording may need feedback on clarity and one recurring phrase pattern; a formal email draft may need attention to tone and organisation. Trying to correct every feature can hide the priority.

Practical application

Use a two-step comment: name the successful move, then ask for one targeted revision. For example: “Your recommendation is clear. Now record it again using one of the hedging phrases from the model.”

Peer feedback needs structure

Online peer feedback can create additional readers and response opportunities, but it needs criteria and modelling. A 2023 meta-analysis comparing online and offline peer feedback in higher education is useful context; it does not mean that any peer-comment activity will improve language learning.

Keep the human judgement

Automated suggestions can help learners notice possible errors, but their accuracy, explanation and suitability need checking. Teachers should avoid presenting a tool’s output as a final judgement about a learner’s language.

Online Teaching

Online education methodologies: a practical framework for language learning

Online lessons work best when their method is designed around a learning goal—not around the platform’s most visible features.

Published August 29, 2026 · 12 min read
Online Teaching

“Online education” is a delivery setting, not a method. A video call, a learning platform and a shared document can support excellent learning—or merely move a lecture onto a screen. The difference lies in the sequence of thinking, practice, feedback and reflection that the course makes possible.

Evidence

A U.S. Department of Education review found that learners in online conditions often performed modestly better than face-to-face comparators. The report also warned that blended conditions commonly included extra time and instructional elements, so the result should not be read as evidence that a medium alone causes better learning.

The four design questions

Before choosing an activity, decide what learners should be able to do, what evidence would show that they can do it, how they will practise, and where they will receive information that helps them improve. These questions work for a short speaking lesson and for a longer self-paced course.

Design questionOnline implication
What is the outcome?State a communicative task, not only a topic.
What needs attention?Break input into purposeful, manageable steps.
Where is practice?Build retrieval, rehearsal, interaction and production into the sequence.
How will improvement happen?Plan feedback and a chance to use it.

A useful lens: presence

The Community of Inquiry framework distinguishes teaching presence (clear design and facilitation), social presence (learners recognising one another as real people) and cognitive presence (making meaning through reflection and discourse). It is a planning lens, not a checklist. A warm welcome without a worthwhile problem to discuss is not enough; neither is a well-written task without a clear teacher presence.

Practical application

For an online English lesson on giving advice: model two useful phrases, let pairs compare two short scenarios in a breakout room, collect options in a shared board, then ask each learner to record one personalised recommendation. The teacher’s feedback is followed by a second attempt.

Synchronous, asynchronous and blended

Live sessions are valuable when learners need immediate interaction, coaching or shared decision-making. Asynchronous work is often better for noticing, drafting, revisiting input and giving considered responses. A coherent programme uses each mode for what it does well instead of trying to make every task happen live.

Common misconception

More technology does not automatically mean more participation. A tool earns its place when it changes what learners can notice, practise, create or share.

How this relates to Language Ticket

Language Ticket’s working approach is to treat the online classroom as a place for purposeful communication and responsive teaching. Research can inform the choices, but a teacher still needs to read the group, adapt the task and decide what feedback will help next.

Key takeaways

  • Start with the learning outcome, not the tool.
  • Design for a cycle of input, practice, feedback and another attempt.
  • Use live and asynchronous time for different kinds of learning.
  • Treat research findings as context-sensitive guidance, not scripts.

  3. Gao et al. (2021), Cognitive Presence in Online Learning: A Systematic Review ↗

Online Teaching

When not to use another edtech tool

A ten-minute evidence-informed article on know when not to add another tool in online English teaching.

Published April 17, 2026 · 10 min read
Online Teaching

When not to use another edtech tool is not a technique that works independently of the people and conditions around it. In online English teaching, the central design question is usually simple: what should learners be able to do differently after this sequence, and what would count as convincing evidence? That question prevents a familiar trap—mistaking activity for learning because the room, screen or worksheet looks busy.

Define the decision before choosing the resource

A resource can be excellent and still be the wrong first move. Before selecting one, write a single sentence that names the decision learners will make. It might be how to organise a response, how to notice a recurring language choice, or how to choose between two options for a real audience. That sentence creates a boundary: anything that does not help learners make the decision can be shortened, delayed or removed.

This does not require a rigid lesson script. It gives teachers and learners a shared purpose. A group may arrive at different language, examples or strategies, but they should know what the task is asking them to work out. When the target is visible, feedback can address the work rather than the person.

Build a sequence that permits a second attempt

The first attempt should reveal something worth responding to. Use a brief model, a worked example, an information gap or a short rehearsal to help people enter the task. Then make the first attempt small: a thirty-second explanation, a paragraph outline, a recorded answer, or a decision with reasons. The smaller the first attempt, the easier it is to notice one useful thing and try again.

The second attempt is where a routine earns its place. It can change the audience, constraint, evidence or language support while preserving the core performance. That makes improvement visible without pretending that one revised answer proves mastery. It also gives learners a reason to use feedback rather than merely receive it.

Allow the tool to be unnecessary

A method or tool should earn its place by improving a defined opportunity to learn. If a simpler route protects attention, access or dialogue, that is evidence for choosing the simpler route.

Do not correct everything at once

A first attempt can reveal several issues, but a second attempt needs a manageable focus. Decide whether the next version is primarily about message, organisation, interaction, accuracy or pronunciation. When feedback tries to address every issue, learners often see a marked-up product rather than a usable next move. A narrow focus can be more demanding because it asks for deliberate control.

A concrete scenario

For online English teaching, ask learners to produce a first response to a realistic prompt. Give feedback on one decision only—clarity, evidence, turn-taking, organisation or language precision. Then change one condition and ask for a second response. Keep both versions. The comparison is often more useful than a score.

Read evidence with appropriate caution

Research can sharpen a question, but it cannot select a lesson for a particular class. Studies differ in participants, duration, setting, outcome measures and the amount of teacher support involved. A review may show a pattern across many studies; it does not guarantee that the same result will appear with every learner, topic or platform. Treat an evidence source as a reason to test a design choice thoughtfully, not as a slogan.

The sources below have different jobs. Some describe learning or assessment principles; others report research syntheses or policy guidance. None should be used to claim more than it supports. A closing question for online learning

A five-minute field guide to end with a better follow-up question in online English teaching.

Published April 7, 2026 · 5 min read
Online Teaching

A closing question for online learning is most useful when it solves a recognizable teaching or learning problem. Start with a real moment: learners hesitate before speaking, a task produces thin answers, or the same homework disappears into a folder. The point is not to add another routine. It is to make the next attempt easier to begin and more informative to review.

Start with one observable change

For online English teaching, choose one behaviour that a learner or teacher could notice within a week. “Improve confidence” is too broad to guide a decision. “Prepare two follow-up questions before a discussion” is observable. Keep the first version modest enough that people can do it without a long explanation or a new platform.

Use the routine in a real task

A routine becomes meaningful when it is attached to a genuine task. In an English lesson, that might mean planning language for a short recommendation, comparing two options, or recording a clearer second response. Show what a useful outcome looks like, but leave room for learners to make the language their own.

A useful test

If learners could complete exactly the same exercise without making a language, study or teaching decision, the routine probably needs more purpose. Add an audience, an information gap, a choice, or a short explanation of why the answer matters. This is not about making every activity elaborate. It is about giving attention somewhere useful to go.

For a learner working alone, the same principle applies. Replacing “review vocabulary” with “write three messages that need these phrases” gives practice a direction. For a teacher, replacing “check understanding” with “ask learners to choose and justify the better option” produces clearer evidence.

End while the evidence is still visible

A short closing question gives learners a chance to name what changed and gives teachers a clue about what to revisit. It is more useful than asking only whether they enjoyed the task.

Try this this week

Make a two-column note: “what happened” and “what I will change next time.” Add one sentence after a task. After three entries, look for a pattern rather than judging a single lesson or study session.

Check the cost as well as the benefit

Ask what the routine asks of attention, time and access. A productive choice for one group can become noise for another if the instructions are dense, the tool is unfamiliar, or the pace leaves no time to think. Adjust the support before deciding that learners are not engaged.

It is also worth checking the emotional cost. A public correction, an open microphone or a timed response can provide useful evidence for some learners and unnecessary pressure for others. Make the purpose explicit, offer preparation where it helps, and interpret a quiet response carefully rather than treating it as an absence of learning.

A small next step

  • Name the performance you want to see.
  • Run the routine once in a meaningful task.
  • Collect one small piece of evidence.
  • Decide what to keep, change or remove.

Review without overreacting

One lesson, conversation or study session rarely gives a complete answer. Look for repeated signals: the same instruction being misunderstood, the same language support being used, or the same point at which learners lose momentum. A short note after several attempts is more reliable than a strong impression from one unusually easy or difficult day.

If the routine is helping, make it simpler rather than larger. If it is not helping, identify whether the problem is the task, the support, the timing or the expectation. Removing an unnecessary step is a valid improvement. Good practice becomes sustainable when it respects the limited attention of teachers and learners.

Online teaching is most useful when it changes what a learner can do, not only what a lesson looks like. This guide offers a practical way to decide where attention belongs, what evidence to collect and how to plan a meaningful next attempt.
