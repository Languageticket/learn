Speaking & Pronunciation

Intelligibility before accent reduction

A useful pronunciation goal is being easy to understand—not erasing every trace of an accent.

Published September 5, 2026 · 6 min read
Speaking & Pronunciation

Intelligibility before accent reduction works best when pronunciation is treated as part of communication rather than as a hunt for a perfect accent. The learner needs a clear feature to notice, a model that is easy to compare with their own production, and several chances to use the feature inside meaningful speech.

Make the target visible

Pronunciation becomes teachable when the target is narrow enough to hear and practise. A learner may understand the idea immediately but still need repeated perception and production before the new pattern becomes available during spontaneous speech.

A practical teaching sequence

  • Start with a short listening contrast or model.
  • Ask learners to identify the feature before producing it.
  • Use a brief controlled rehearsal with clear feedback.
  • Move quickly into a sentence or interaction where meaning matters.
  • Return to the same feature later instead of trying to perfect it in one lesson.
Try this

Use a 60–90 second task built around intelligibility before accent reduction. Give learners one clear success criterion, let them try once, provide feedback on that criterion only, then change the partner, prompt or condition and ask for a second attempt. Save or note both versions so the improvement is visible.

Keep feedback narrow

Choose one priority for the next attempt. A learner can work on clarity, interaction, stress, a sound contrast or one repair strategy, but trying to fix every visible problem at once usually makes the second attempt less useful.

What progress can look like

Progress should be judged by whether the learner is easier to understand, more consistent, or more able to notice and self-correct the feature. A short before-and-after recording is often more useful than a vague judgement such as 'better accent'.

What to avoid

Do not treat one accent as the only legitimate target. The most useful priority is normally intelligibility and communicative effectiveness, while still respecting the learner's identity and goals.

Sources

  2. Derwing & Munro (2005) — Second Language Accent and Pronunciation Teaching ↗
  3. British Council TeachingEnglish — Teaching speaking: Individual Sounds ↗

Speaking & Pronunciation

Pausing that supports fluency

Strategic pauses can make speech sound more controlled even when a learner is still planning.

Published August 30, 2026 · 5 min read
Speaking & Pronunciation

Pausing that supports fluency is a speaking skill, not only a phrase list. Learners need a reason to use it while listening, planning and responding in real time. The task should make the strategy useful rather than asking learners to display it for the teacher.

Make the target visible

Speaking places several demands on attention at once: deciding what to say, choosing language, monitoring the listener and keeping the interaction moving. A well-designed task reduces unnecessary load while leaving the communicative decision intact.

A practical teaching sequence

  • Give learners a clear outcome, not only a topic.
  • Provide a few useful phrases or examples without scripting the whole exchange.
  • Allow brief planning or rehearsal before the first attempt.
  • Monitor for one or two patterns instead of correcting everything.
  • Repeat the task with a small change so learners can use feedback.
Try this

Use a 60–90 second task built around pausing that supports fluency. Give learners one clear success criterion, let them try once, provide feedback on that criterion only, then change the partner, prompt or condition and ask for a second attempt. Save or note both versions so the improvement is visible.

Keep feedback narrow

Choose one priority for the next attempt. A learner can work on clarity, interaction, stress, a sound contrast or one repair strategy, but trying to fix every visible problem at once usually makes the second attempt less useful.

What progress can look like

Useful evidence includes longer turns, clearer responses, better listener engagement, fewer communication breakdowns, or a more successful second attempt. The goal is not simply more words; it is more effective interaction.

What to avoid

Avoid turning a fluency activity into a constant correction exercise. If every sentence is interrupted, learners may become more careful but less willing to communicate.

Assessing speaking without rewarding accent imitation

Speaking assessment should focus on communication, control and intelligibility rather than similarity to one prestige accent.

Published July 20, 2026 · 7 min read
Speaking & Pronunciation

Assessing speaking without rewarding accent imitation should be evaluated through evidence of real communication. A useful speaking or pronunciation measure looks at what the learner can do, how understandable the message is, and whether the learner can adapt after feedback.

Make the target visible

A single score can hide important change. Two learners may receive the same result while showing different strengths in interaction, fluency, intelligibility, range or control.

A practical teaching sequence

  • Collect a short baseline sample.
  • Choose one or two visible success criteria.
  • Give learners a chance to rehearse and perform.
  • Add feedback that leads to another attempt.
  • Keep samples over time so improvement can be compared.
Try this

Use a 60–90 second task built around assessing speaking without rewarding accent imitation. Give learners one clear success criterion, let them try once, provide feedback on that criterion only, then change the partner, prompt or condition and ask for a second attempt. Save or note both versions so the improvement is visible.

Keep feedback narrow

Choose one priority for the next attempt. A learner can work on clarity, interaction, stress, a sound contrast or one repair strategy, but trying to fix every visible problem at once usually makes the second attempt less useful.

What progress can look like

A portfolio, recording sequence or short analytic rubric can make progress visible without pretending that every aspect of speaking changes at the same speed.

What to avoid

Do not reward imitation of a prestige accent as if it were the same thing as communicative competence. Assessment should remain connected to understandable and effective language use.
